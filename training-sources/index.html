KORD training sources

The studies and coaching references behind the rules in KORD running plans. In the app, each reason on a plan session, an adjusted run or a taper week carries a numbered footnote that opens the source.

Each source is re-checked on the same schedule as the medical sources · sources.json

General training information, not medical advice. Stop and seek advice if you have pain or feel unwell.

How much running is too much? Identifying high-risk running sessions

Frandsen et al. · British Journal of Sports Medicine · 2025
  • What they studied: 5,205 adult runners followed for 18 months, with about 588,000 runs recorded on Garmin watches.
  • What they found: Injuries rose when a single run was more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days. Runs 10–30% longer had 1.64× the injury rate; runs more than twice as long had 2.28×.
  • How KORD uses it: No planned run is more than 10% longer than your longest run in the 30 days before it, and the Training injury projection counts any run that is.

Excessive progression in weekly running distance and risk of running-related injuries

Nielsen et al. · Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy · 2014
  • What they studied: 874 novice runners with GPS watches, followed for a year.
  • What they found: Runners who raised their weekly distance by more than 30% had more injuries than those under 10% (hazard ratio 1.59), but the difference was not statistically significant.
  • How KORD uses it: Weekly miles rise at most 8% on your last full week, with a lighter week every 4th week. The Training injury projection counts it as weaker evidence than single long runs.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis

Bosquet et al. · Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise · 2007
  • What they studied: 27 studies of competitive athletes, comparing how tapers were done.
  • What they found: The best tapers lasted about 2 weeks and cut training volume by 41–60%, while keeping intensity and how often athletes trained.
  • How KORD uses it: Taper weeks cut miles step by step but keep all your runs and some marathon-pace work.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Longer disciplined tapers improve marathon performance for recreational runners

Smyth & Lawlor · Frontiers in Sports and Active Living · 2021
  • What they studied: The training of about 158,000 recreational marathon runners before their races.
  • What they found: Strict 3-week tapers were linked to the biggest gain: a median 5 min 32 s (2.6%) faster than a minimal taper. Strict 2-week tapers came close.
  • How KORD uses it: Your plan tapers for 3 weeks: −17%, −35%, then −50% from your peak week.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Training paces from current fitness (VDOT)

Jack Daniels · Daniels' Running Formula · VDOT O2
  • What they studied: A coaching system that sets easy, marathon and threshold paces from a recent race or time trial.
  • What they found: To improve at a safe rate, train at the paces your current fitness gives, not your goal pace, and update them as you get fitter.
  • How KORD uses it: Paces come from your Race times forecast, refreshed as you run. Your goal pace is used only when it is slower.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

How long should the long run be?

Daniels; Hansons Marathon Method · coaching guidance
  • What they studied: Widely used marathon coaching methods.
  • What they found: Both keep the long run to about 25–30% of weekly mileage; other coaches allow up to about 35%.
  • How KORD uses it: On four run days the long run can reach about 40% of the week, so the plan adds a 5th easy run once your week passes 35 mi and you have a free day.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Individual endurance training prescription with heart rate variability

Vesterinen et al. · Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise · 2016
  • What they studied: 40 recreational endurance runners over 8 weeks: a fixed plan, or hard sessions only when morning HRV was in its normal range.
  • What they found: The HRV-guided group did fewer moderate and hard sessions (13.2 vs 17.7) and still improved its fitness.
  • How KORD uses it: When recovery is low on the morning of a hard run, KORD offers an easy run instead. Nothing changes unless you choose it.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Endurance training guided individually by daily heart rate variability measurements

Kiviniemi et al. · European Journal of Applied Physiology · 2007
  • What they studied: 26 moderately fit men: a fixed plan, HRV-guided training, or no training.
  • What they found: Training hard only when morning HRV had not dropped improved maximal running speed and VO₂ peak.
  • How KORD uses it: When recovery is low on the morning of a hard run, KORD offers an easy run instead. Nothing changes unless you choose it.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

The effectiveness of exercise interventions to prevent sports injuries

Lauersen et al. · British Journal of Sports Medicine · 2014
  • What they studied: 25 randomised trials with 26,610 participants.
  • What they found: Strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third and nearly halved overuse injuries.
  • How KORD uses it: Your plan includes two optional 25-minute lower-body strength sessions a week.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Prevention, diagnosis and treatment of the overtraining syndrome

Meeusen et al. · Joint consensus statement of the ECSS and ACSM · Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise · 2013
  • What they studied: An expert consensus from the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine.
  • What they found: Training must avoid too much load combined with too little recovery. No single marker reliably shows when that is happening.
  • How KORD uses it: A lighter week is only offered after several low-recovery mornings across HRV, resting heart rate and sleep, never after one bad reading. Nothing changes unless you choose it.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Incidence of running-related injuries per 1000 h of running in different types of runners

Videbæk et al. · Sports Medicine · 2015 (systematic review and meta-analysis)
  • What they studied: Studies reporting running injuries per hour of running, grouped by type of runner.
  • What they found: Recreational runners had 7.7 running-related injuries per 1,000 hours of running (95% CI 6.9–8.7).
  • How KORD uses it: The base rate behind the Training injury projection for running.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Run Clever: progressing running intensity or running volume (randomised trial)

Ramskov et al. · BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine · 2018
  • What they studied: Recreational runners randomised to build their training mainly by intensity or mainly by volume.
  • What they found: Progressing running intensity did not injure more runners than progressing volume.
  • How KORD uses it: Pace and intensity are flagged in the Training injury projection but not counted in the percentage.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Injury in professional road cycling, per 1,000 hours

Fallon et al. · Sports · 2025
  • What they studied: Injuries among professional road cyclists in training and racing.
  • What they found: In training, 1.23 injuries per 1,000 hours (95% CI 0.8–1.7).
  • How KORD uses it: The base rate behind the Training injury projection for cycling.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Injury incidence in resistance training (systematic review)

Serafim et al. · Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research · 2023
  • What they studied: Studies of injuries across forms of resistance training.
  • What they found: Injury incidence ranged from 0.21 to 18.9 per 1,000 hours; traditional strength training was the safest form.
  • How KORD uses it: The Training injury projection uses 1 injury per 1,000 hours of strength training.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Strength training as superior, dose-dependent and safe prevention of acute and overuse sports injuries

Lauersen et al. · British Journal of Sports Medicine · 2018 (meta-analysis)
  • What they studied: Randomised trials of strength training to prevent sports injuries.
  • What they found: Strength training cut injuries to about a third, and each 10% more strength-training volume reduced injury risk by more than four percentage points.
  • How KORD uses it: More strength training never raises the Training injury projection.
  • Open the source · last checked 2026-11-27

Shoulder pain in elite swimmers: primarily due to swim-volume-induced supraspinatus tendinopathy

Sein et al. · British Journal of Sports Medicine · 2010
  • What they studied: Elite swimmers: training volume, shoulder pain and imaging of the shoulder.
  • What they found: Hours and distance swum per week were linked to supraspinatus tendinopathy. There is no per-hour injury rate.
  • How KORD uses it: Swimming shows load flags in the Training injury projection, but no percentage.
